Quality: The methodology is sensible but thin where it matters most for a benchmark paper. The core experimental loop — problems → workspace with affordances → multiple detection methods → human gold standard — is well-designed. However, the sample size used for benchmarking is small, and variance across different seeds is not reported. The gap between Claude and Codex/Gemini in Table 1 may also have shifted with newer, stronger Claude models that were not benchmarked. Section 2.1 mentions that the problems were adapted to elicit reward hacking but provides no further details. The paper would be stronger with a sensitivity analysis using different random seeds and holdout sets to check robustness to those choices. The LLM-as-judge evaluation also suffers from small sample sizes.

Clarity: The paper is easy to follow. Tables 1 and 2 are well-organized, and Figure 1 (capability vs. hacking rate) is the most informative plot. Several typos remain ("Sonent" for Sonnet, "beahvior", "exceprt", "soluion").

Originality/Significance: This is a useful but marginally incremental contribution. The core differentiator — studying reward hacking on solvable rather than artificially impossible problems — is genuine, and the head-to-head comparison of detection methods against human ground truth is the clearest contribution. That said, the domain (competitive programming with writable test files) is narrow, and the authors acknowledge that LLM judges perform much worse on SWE-bench (per ImpossibleBench), so the positive findings here likely do not transfer to realistic agentic deployments.
